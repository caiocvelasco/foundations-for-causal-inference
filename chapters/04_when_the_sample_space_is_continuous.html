Real Life: When the Sample Space Is Continuous

In the previous chapter, we constructed a probability space

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal F,P) \]

to represent a random experiment mathematically.

So far, our examples have had simple sample spaces: heads or tails, purchase or no purchase, the faces of a die.

But many quantities used in Data Science — such as time, temperature, distance, or spending — are often modeled as continuous.

When this happens, a new difficulty arises:

How do we construct probabilities when we can no longer list all the possible outcomes?

This is the focus of this chapter.

Before moving on: finite and infinite

Before discussing continuous spaces, we need to establish a simple distinction.

A set can be:

  • finite: it contains a finite number of elements;
  • infinite: it contains infinitely many elements.

For example,

\[ \{H,T\} \]

is finite, whereas

\[ \mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\} \]

is infinite.

But there is an important distinction between infinite sets: some can still be organized into a sequence; others cannot.

Counting, listing, and sequences

Counting and cardinality

Counting means putting the elements of a set into correspondence with the natural numbers.

In this course, we will use

\[ \mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}. \]

For example, for

\[ A=\{a,b,c\}, \]

we can associate

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} a & b & c\\ \uparrow & \uparrow & \uparrow\\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{array} \]

and we say that

\[ |A|=3. \]

This quantity is the cardinality of the set.

For infinite sets, counting never ends. The question then becomes:

Can we organize all the elements into sequential positions indexed by \(1,2,3,\ldots\), without leaving any out?

What does it mean to list a set?

A sequence is precisely a collection organized by position:

\[ x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots \]

So, in this chapter, saying that we can list a set means:

we can construct a sequence that contains all the elements of the set.

We do not need to finish writing the list. We simply need a rule specifying what occupies position \(1\), position \(2\), position \(3\), and so on.

When this is possible, the set is countable.

Some infinite sets we can list

The first example is almost immediate.

The positive integers can be listed as

\[ 1,2,3,4,5,\ldots \]

Now consider all the integers,

\[ \mathbb Z=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. \]

At first glance, it seems that we now have “more” numbers on both sides. But we can still organize them into a single sequence:

\[ 0,\;1,\;-1,\;2,\;-2,\;3,\;-3,\ldots \]

Or, thinking in terms of positions,

\[ \begin{array}{ccccccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & \cdots\\ \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 2 & -2 & 3 & \cdots \end{array} \]

Therefore, \(\mathbb Z\) is also countable.

The same is true of the rational numbers,

\[ \mathbb Q = \left\{ \frac{p}{q}:p,q\in\mathbb Z,\;q\neq0 \right\}. \]

For example,

\[ \frac12,\quad -\frac34,\quad \frac57,\quad 2,\quad -\frac{11}{3} \]

are all elements of \(\mathbb Q\).

Here the list is less obvious, because there are infinitely many rational numbers between two integers.

Even so, we can organize them systematically.

For example, one possible sequence starts like this:

\[ q_1=0,\qquad q_2=1,\qquad q_3=-1,\qquad q_4=\frac12,\qquad q_5=-\frac12,\qquad q_6=2,\qquad q_7=-2,\qquad \ldots \]

or, more visually,

\[ 0,\; 1,\; -1,\; \frac12,\; -\frac12,\; 2,\; -2,\; \frac13,\; -\frac13,\; \frac23,\; -\frac23,\; \ldots \]

The exact order is not the main point.

What matters is that there is some enumeration rule that allows us to put all the rational numbers into a sequence

\[ q_1,q_2,q_3,\ldots \]

without leaving out any rational number.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\mathbb Q\text{ is infinite but countable}.} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\mathbb N,\;\mathbb Z\;\text{and}\;\mathbb Q\text{ are infinite but countable}.} \]

This is one of the first surprising things we encounter when studying infinity: sets that seem much larger can still have their elements organized into a sequence.

Can every infinite set be listed?

No.

Cantor showed that there are infinite sets too large to fit into any sequence

\[ x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots \]

These sets are called uncountable.

The example that matters to us is precisely

\[ [0,1]. \]

In a moment, we will see a short and beautiful proof of this.

The classification we will use

\[ \boxed{ \text{sets} \longrightarrow \begin{cases} \text{finite}\\ \text{infinite} \begin{cases} \text{countable}\\ \text{uncountable} \end{cases} \end{cases} } \]

The idea of a sequence will be fundamental because Kolmogorov’s third axiom allows us to sum probabilities over a countable collection of events.

When we cannot list the outcomes

We can now isolate the new difficulty.

Consider

\[ \Omega=[0,1]. \]

The new question is this: can we list all the points of \([0,1]\) as

\[ \omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots \; ? \]

The answer is no.

More precisely, sets such as \((0,1)\) and \([0,1]\) are uncountable.

This means that there is no infinite list

\[ \omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots \]

that captures all their elements.

Let’s look at a short and beautiful proof from Tsitsiklis’s lecture.

An intuitive proof by contradiction

A proof by contradiction works as follows:

  1. we temporarily assume that the statement we want to negate is true;
  2. we follow the logical consequences of that assumption;
  3. if we arrive at an absurdity or impossibility, we conclude that the initial assumption was wrong.

In very simple terms:

“Let’s pretend for a moment that this can be done.
If that assumption leads us to a dead end, then it was not actually possible.”

Cantor’s proof: why \([0,1]\) cannot be listed

Rather than looking at all the numbers in \([0,1]\) at once, let’s focus on a specific subset:

\[ A=\{x\in[0,1]: \text{ the decimal expansion of }x\text{ uses only the digits }2\text{ and }7\}. \]

There is nothing special about using 2 and 7; this is simply an example sufficient for what we want to do here.

For example, some elements of \(A\) are:

\[ 0.2222\ldots,\quad 0.72727\ldots,\quad 0.277772\ldots,\quad 0.7722227\ldots \]

If even this special set could be listed, then we could write something like this:

\[ x_1,\;x_2,\;x_3,\;\ldots \]

with all the elements of \(A\) appearing somewhere in the list.

Let’s assume that this is possible.

Step 1: Assuming it is possible, we start listing some of these numbers

Imagine a list like this:

x1 = 0. 2 7 2 7 7 2 …
x2 = 0. 7 2 7 2 7 7 …
x3 = 0. 2 2 7 7 2 7 …
x4 = 0. 7 7 2 2 7 2 …
…

We then ask ourselves:

  • Can this sequence exhaust all the possible elements of \(A\)?

Now look at the first decimal digit of \(x_1\), the second decimal digit of \(x_2\), the third decimal digit of \(x_3\), and so on.

These digits form what is called the diagonal of the list.

Step 2: The key trick: construct a new number that could belong to the sequence

Now let’s construct a new number \(y\) of the form: \(y=0.y_1y_2y_3\ldots\)

The construction rule is very simple:

  • look at the first decimal digit of \(x_1\), which is \(2\), and replace it with \(7\)
  • look at the second decimal digit of \(x_2\), which is \(2\), and replace it with \(7\)
  • look at the third decimal digit of \(x_3\), which is \(7\), and replace it with \(2\)
  • and so on

Focusing on the highlighted diagonal, for example, if the diagonal of the list were

2, 2, 7, 2, ...

then we would construct

7, 7, 2, 7, ...

In the end, following this rule of Cantor’s diagonal argument, we obtain a number \(y=0.7727\ldots\).

Step 3: Would this number appear in the sequence?

Notice that this new number \(y\) still belongs to \(A\), because its decimal expansion also uses only the digits \(2\) and \(7\).

But it cannot equal any of the numbers in the list, as we can see below:

  • \(y\neq x_1\), because it differs from \(x_1\) in the first decimal digit;
  • \(y\neq x_2\), because it differs from \(x_2\) in the second decimal digit;
  • \(y\neq x_3\), because it differs from \(x_3\) in the third decimal digit;
  • and so on.

In general, we have just discovered that

\[ y\neq x_i \quad \text{for every }i. \]

In other words, we constructed an element of \(A\) that does not appear in the list!

Following our proof by contradiction, we started from the assumption that we could list all the elements of \(A\) in a sequence, but ended up with a conclusion contradicting the assumption that the list contained all the elements of \(A\).

Step 4: Conclusion

Thus, our initial assumption was wrong.

Therefore, the set \(A\) is uncountable.

Since \(A\subset[0,1]\), we conclude that the interval \([0,1]\) itself cannot be countable.

The takeaway from the proof

Even a very special subset of \([0,1]\) is already too large to fit into a list.

All the more reason why the entire interval cannot be enumerated.

What about the entire set of real numbers, \(\mathbb R\)?

Proving this directly for the entire set of real numbers would require a little more organization.

But conceptually, the essential difficulty has already appeared here.

If the interval \([0,1]\), which is contained in \(\mathbb R\), is already uncountable, then the set of all real numbers cannot be countable either.

What matters to us in this chapter is the probabilistic consequence:

when the sample space is a continuum such as \([0,1]\), we cannot treat its points as a sequence \(\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots\).

And this is exactly what will come up when we try to use Kolmogorov’s third axiom for something very interesting later on.

The strange part: the whole has probability \(1\), but each point has probability \(0\)

Before moving into the continuous case, it is worth revisiting an infinite case in which everything still works in a familiar way.

Imagine the experiment:

toss a coin repeatedly until the first heads appears.

If we record the toss on which the first heads appears, we have \(\Omega=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}.\)

This sample space is infinite but countable: we can organize all possible outcomes into a list \(1,2,3,4,\ldots\)

Each individual outcome corresponds to a singleton, \(\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\ldots,\) and if we model the coin as fair, we are choosing a specific probability law for this experiment, according to which

\[ P(\{1\})=\frac12,\qquad P(\{2\})=\frac14,\qquad P(\{3\})=\frac18,\qquad\ldots \]

Since these events are mutually exclusive and the collection is countable, Kolmogorov’s third axiom applies directly:

\[ P(\Omega) = P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{n\}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(\{n\}) = \frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots =1. \]

So far, the more “naive” intuition of looking only at individual outcomes when working with Kolmogorov’s third axiom works perfectly: we can think of the space as a list of individual outcomes, assign a probability to each one, and reconstruct larger events by adding these probabilities, without inconsistencies.

But the most important point is not merely that the sum worked out. It is that the function \(P\) remained consistent in this context.

Consider an example.

Suppose we want to ask the following question about the experiment:

“What is the probability that the first heads appears on one of the first three tosses?”

This event can be represented by \(A = \{1,2,3\}\) and, as we know, it is a subset of the sample space.

Since outcomes \(1\), \(2\), and \(3\) are mutually exclusive, we can break this question down into three smaller cases: \(P(\{1\})\), \(P(\{2\})\), and \(P(\{3\})\).

But can we simply add these probabilities? More importantly: is this sum exactly the probability that \(A\) occurs?

In other words, we want to know whether the third axiom really preserves the consistency of the function \(P\) in this case:

\[ P(A)=P(\{1\}) + P(\{2\}) + P(\{3\}). \]

Whether we calculate the probability of \(A\) directly or break the question down into these three individual outcomes and add their probabilities, the answer must be the same.

This is precisely the consistency we want to preserve in the function \(P\).

\(P\) is a function, and the axioms guarantee its internal consistency

We want to use \(P\) to answer questions about a random experiment.

Each question corresponds to an event \(A\subseteq\Omega\), and the function

\[ P(A) \]

assigns a number to that event.

Kolmogorov’s three axioms are not there to tell us, on their own, which numbers each event should receive. They tell us how a valid probability function must behave.

The three requirements make sense when we think about the real-world phenomenon:

  • probabilities cannot be negative;
  • something in \(\Omega\) necessarily occurs, so \(P(\Omega)=1\);
  • if a question can be broken down into mutually exclusive possibilities, calculating the probability of the whole or of its parts cannot produce incompatible answers.

It is this last requirement that will now be decisive.

Suppose a broader event can occur in two mutually exclusive ways:

\[ A=A_1\cup A_2, \qquad A_1\cap A_2=\varnothing. \]

Then the internal consistency of the function \(P\) requires

\[ P(A)=P(A_1)+P(A_2). \]

In terms of the experiment: if we ask “did \(A_1\) or \(A_2\) occur?”, the answer cannot change simply because we decided to break that broader question into two smaller possibilities.

And if the decomposition has infinitely many parts that can still be arranged in a sequence,

\[ A=A_1\cup A_2\cup A_3\cup\cdots, \]

with the \(A_i\) mutually exclusive, we want to preserve exactly the same consistency:

\[ P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(A_n). \]

This is Kolmogorov’s third axiom: countable additivity.

In the coin example, it applies directly to the singletons themselves because \(\Omega\) is countable.

And this is where the key insight will come in when we switch to a continuous sample space — keep this in mind!

Now we return to the experiment we are interested in:

draw a number between \(0\) and \(1\) at random.

Our sample space is

\[ \Omega=[0,1]. \]

As we have just proved using Cantor’s argument, this space is also infinite, but it is now uncountable: this means that there is no sequence

\[ \omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots \]

that contains all its possible outcomes.

This is where the story changes.

This is where measure theory comes in

So far, in countable spaces, we have been able to think of individual outcomes as the basic building blocks: we assign probabilities to singletons and reconstruct larger events by adding those probabilities.

But in \(\Omega=[0,1]\) this is no longer possible point by point, because there is no list containing all the outcomes.

A natural question then arises:

How can we continue assigning probabilities to events consistently if we can no longer construct everything by adding the probabilities of individual outcomes?

This is precisely where modern probability theory borrows the framework of measure theory.

The idea is simple and very powerful: rather than requiring the size of a set always to be constructed by adding the sizes of its points, we can assign a size directly to sets, provided that this assignment obeys consistency rules.

A measure is, intuitively, a function that assigns a “size” to sets. Length measures intervals, area measures regions of the plane, and in probability theory, \(P\) assigns probability mass to events.

Probability is therefore a special case of a measure, normalized so that

\[ P(\Omega)=1. \]

Kolmogorov’s axioms formalize precisely the properties that this function \(P\) must satisfy to remain consistent.

The most important conceptual point is:

we do not need to define the probability of an event as the sum of the probabilities of all the points that make it up.

We can assign probability directly to sets — to events — and require these assignments to be compatible with one another, especially when we break an event into a countable sequence of mutually exclusive parts.

Later, we will formalize which sets can be assigned this probability measure by introducing \(\mathcal F\), measurability, and \(\sigma\)-algebras. For now, we only want to understand why this change in perspective is necessary in the continuous case.

\(\Omega=[0,1]\) cannot be listed point by point

Since \([0,1]\) consists of all its points, in set theory we can certainly write

\[ [0,1] = \bigcup_{\omega\in[0,1]}\{\omega\}. \]

There is no problem so far. In other words, the union of all its points is the entire set, of course.

The problem arises if we try to treat this collection of singletons as a sequence

\[ \{\omega_1\},\{\omega_2\},\{\omega_3\},\ldots \]

because that would require the existence of a list \(\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots\) containing all the points of \([0,1]\).

And we have just proved that this is impossible.

The collection

\[ \{\{\omega\}:\omega\in[0,1]\} \]

is also uncountable.

Therefore, the third axiom does not authorize us to decompose the space point by point and turn this union into a (countable) sum of probabilities, as would be justified by countable additivity.

🤨 So how does the third axiom still work here?

Measure theory has already given us the main conceptual shift: \(P\) is defined on events and does not need to be constructed by adding probabilities point by point.

Now we want to understand how countable additivity continues to preserve the consistency of \(P\) when we break a larger event into smaller parts.

This is the key point: we do not need to partition \(\Omega\) into singletons. We can partition an uncountable space into a countable collection of disjoint subsets, even if each of these subsets still contains uncountably many points.

The key insight: we enumerate the blocks, not the points

For example, consider

\[ A_1=(1/2,1],\qquad A_2=(1/4,1/2],\qquad A_3=(1/8,1/4],\qquad\ldots \]

and, to complete \([0,1]\), we add the singleton \(\{0\}\).

We can visualize this decomposition directly on the number line:

0        1/8        1/4              1/2                         1
●---------|----------|----------------|---------------------------|
   ...        A3              A2                     A1

where

\[ A_1=(1/2,1],\qquad A_2=(1/4,1/2],\qquad A_3=(1/8,1/4],\qquad\ldots \]

and the point \(0\) is included separately as the singleton \(\{0\}\).

Visually, we are simply dividing \([0,1]\) into disjoint blocks:

\[ [0,1] = \{0\} \cup \cdots \cup A_3 \cup A_2 \cup A_1. \]

Notice what happened.

The sample space is still \(\Omega=[0,1],\) so it is still uncountable.

Moreover, each interval \(A_i\) also contains uncountably many points.

What changed was something else: we managed to organize the blocks of the partition into a sequence.

In fact, there is a simple rule for generating each one:

\[ A_n = \left( \frac{1}{2^n}, \frac{1}{2^{\,n-1}} \right], \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots \]

Observe:

\[ n=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A_1=(1/2,1], \]

\[ n=2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A_2=(1/4,1/2], \]

\[ n=3 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A_3=(1/8,1/4], \]

and so on.

In other words, we can put each block into correspondence with a natural number:

\[ 1\longleftrightarrow A_1,\qquad 2\longleftrightarrow A_2,\qquad 3\longleftrightarrow A_3,\qquad\ldots \]

This is exactly what it means to say that this collection is countable.

The singleton \(\{0\}\) poses no problem: we simply include it as one more block of the partition. For example, we can call it \(A_0\).

Thus,

\[ [0,1] = A_0\cup A_1\cup A_2\cup A_3\cup\cdots, \qquad A_0=\{0\}. \]

Moreover, these blocks do not overlap: no outcome can belong to two of them at the same time.

Therefore, we have a countable partition of an uncountable space.

This is the key point:

We cannot enumerate the points of \([0,1]\). But we can partition \([0,1]\) into a countable collection of subsets, even if each of these subsets contains uncountably many points.

And now Kolmogorov’s third axiom can be applied directly to this decomposition:

\[ P([0,1]) = P(A_0)+P(A_1)+P(A_2)+P(A_3)+\cdots. \]

The space has not become countable. It is the partition that is countable.

This is the key insight:

we have not made the space \([0,1]\) countable. It remains uncountable. What we have managed to enumerate is the collection of subsets into which we chose to decompose the space.

We now satisfy the conditions of the third axiom.

Since these events are mutually exclusive,

\[ P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(A_n). \]

Therefore, even in an uncountable sample space, we can still break more complex questions into smaller questions and reconstruct the probability of the whole by adding the probabilities of the parts, provided that the family of parts over which we sum is countable.

The central point

In the countable discrete case, we can often use the individual outcomes themselves as the pieces of the decomposition.

In the continuous case, this point-by-point strategy no longer works, because the points form an uncountable family.

But this does not destroy the idea of probability: we continue to assign probability to events — that is, to sets — and continue to require that whenever an event can be decomposed into a countable sequence of mutually exclusive parts, the probability of the whole can be reconstructed by adding the probabilities of those parts.

This is precisely the consistency that the third axiom protects.

But what is the probability of a singleton?

There is an important distinction here.

So far, the axioms have told us how the function \(P\) must behave.

They have not yet told us which numerical values \(P\) should assign to specific events.

In particular, from the three axioms alone, we cannot conclude that

\[ P(\{0.37\})=0. \]

To answer this, we need to take a second step: choose a specific probability law for the experiment.

The axioms provide the structure and internal consistency of the function \(P\); the probability law determines how probability is actually distributed across events.

Let’s now be more specific, that is, give an example of a possible \(P\).

Suppose the number is drawn according to the uniform law on \([0,1]\).

In this model, intervals of equal length have equal probability, and

\[ P([a,b])=b-a, \qquad 0\leq a\leq b\leq1. \]

For example,

\[ P([0.2,0.5])=0.3. \]

Now we can ask:

what is the probability of obtaining exactly \(0.37\)?

Consider increasingly smaller intervals around this point:

\[ A_2=[0.36,0.38], \]

\[ A_3=[0.369,0.371], \]

\[ A_4=[0.3699,0.3701], \]

and so on.

In general,

\[ A_n= \left[ 0.37-\frac{1}{10^n}, 0.37+\frac{1}{10^n} \right]. \]

These sets keep shrinking,

\[ A_2\supset A_3\supset A_4\supset\cdots, \]

and their intersection contains only the point \(0.37\):

\[ \bigcap_{n=2}^{\infty}A_n=\{0.37\}. \]

Since we are using the uniform law, the probability of each interval is its length:

\[ P(A_n)=\frac{2}{10^n}. \]

And therefore,

\[ P(\{0.37\}) = \lim_{n\to\infty}P(A_n) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2}{10^n} =0. \]

Taking this limit is a consequence of countable additivity itself.

Thus, in this model,

\[ \boxed{P(\{\omega\})=0\quad\text{for every }\omega\in[0,1].} \]

Notice where this zero came from.

It did not come simply from the fact that \([0,1]\) is continuous.

It did not come simply from the fact that \([0,1]\) is uncountable.

And it did not come directly from the three axioms.

It came from the uniform law we have just chosen.

Now comes the provocative question

We have, simultaneously,

\[ \boxed{P([0,1])=1} \]

by Kolmogorov’s second axiom, and

\[ \boxed{P(\{\omega\})=0\quad\text{for every }\omega\in[0,1]} \]

by the uniform law we have just specified.

Someone might then reason:

\[ [0,1] = \bigcup_{\omega\in[0,1]}\{\omega\}. \]

“If every point has probability zero, why not add up all those zeros?”

It is tempting to write

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{\omega\in[0,1]}\{\omega\} \right) \overset{\color{red}{?}}{=} \sum_{\omega\in[0,1]}P(\{\omega\}). \]

And then arrive at

\[ 1=0. \]

But now we know exactly where the error lies.

The third axiom guarantees additivity for a countable sequence of disjoint events:

\[ A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots \]

The family of singletons of \([0,1]\) is not countable.

There is no sequence

\[ \omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots \]

that contains all the points of the interval.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \bigcup_{\omega\in[0,1]}\{\omega\} \neq \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\{\omega_i\} } \]

for any sequence \(\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\ldots\).

We have not discovered that \(1=0\).

We have discovered that we tried to use a rule outside the conditions under which it holds.

The strangeness continues: remove a point and the probability is still 1

We can now see why partitioning the space into larger blocks solves precisely the problem that the third axiom needs to address.

In the uniform model,

\[ P(A_1)=\frac12,\qquad P(A_2)=\frac14,\qquad P(A_3)=\frac18,\qquad\ldots \]

Thus,

\[ P((0,1]) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(A_i) = \frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots = 1. \]

Now comes another consequence that seems strange at first glance.

The interval \((0,1]\) differs from \([0,1]\): we removed the point \(0\).

Even so, in this uniform model,

\[ P(\{0\})=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ P([0,1]) = P((0,1])+P(\{0\}) = 1+0 = 1. \]

In other words, removing one possible outcome from the space did not change the total probability.

This may seem contradictory: after all, \([0,1]\) and \((0,1]\) are different sets.

But the function \(P\) is not simply measuring whether two sets contain exactly the same elements. It assigns probability to these events according to the chosen law. And under the uniform law, a singleton such as \(\{0\}\) is assigned probability zero.

Thus,

\[ [0,1]\neq(0,1], \]

but

\[ P([0,1])=P((0,1])=1. \]

This already anticipates an important idea: different events can have the same probability.

Returning to the main point:

  1. point by point, we have an uncountable collection of singletons, so countable additivity cannot be applied in this way;
  2. by grouping the points into a countable partition of disjoint subsets, we can apply the third axiom as usual;
  3. each of these subsets can still contain uncountably many points.

The space can be uncountable. What must be countable is the collection of events over which we sum.

The madness of uncertainty about an individual outcome

Speaking of probability \(0\)

We have reached one of the strangest — and most beautiful — consequences of continuous models.

Consider again the uniform experiment on

\[ \Omega=[0,1]. \]

Choose a specific point, for example

\[ \omega=0.372814\ldots \]

Since a single point has zero length,

\[ P(\{\omega\})=0. \]

So far, so good.

But what if we perform (or realize) the experiment?

Some value in \([0,1]\) will certainly be observed!

Perhaps it will be \(0.372814\ldots\) or some other point.

But whatever value is observed, before the experiment that singleton had probability zero. 😲

In other words:

\[ \boxed{\text{the outcome that actually occurred had probability }0.} \]

This seems absurd the first time we see it.

But it is exactly what happens in a continuous model.

🤯 So something with probability zero actually happened?

Yes.

What occurred in the trial was an individual outcome \(\omega\).

And, in a continuous model like this,

\[ P(\{\omega\})=0 \]

for each individual point.

Therefore, an event having probability zero does not necessarily imply that it is the empty set, that is, that it contains none of the possible outcomes in \(\Omega\):

\[ \boxed{P(A)=0\;\not\Rightarrow\;A=\varnothing.} \]

A singleton such as \(\{0.372814\ldots\}\) is not impossible. It is a perfectly possible outcome of the experiment — it simply has probability measure zero.

This distinction is important.

Another interesting point is that we are not saying that the point has an “infinitesimally small” probability. In this model, \(P(\{\omega\})=0\) exactly.

In experiments with a continuous sample space, a much more useful way to think probabilistically is not to ask about the probability of an exact point, but about the probability that the outcome falls near that point.

In mathematics, we often use \(\varepsilon>0\) to represent a small distance around a number. Thus, around \(\omega\), we can imagine the small interval

                 ω
                 ●
        |--------|--------|
      ω - ε      ω      ω + ε

The smaller \(\varepsilon\) is, the narrower this neighborhood around \(\omega\) becomes.

Instead of asking for the probability of the exact point,

\[ P(X=\omega), \]

we can then ask for the probability that the outcome falls within this small interval:

\[ P(\omega-\varepsilon<X<\omega+\varepsilon). \]

As \(\varepsilon\) decreases, the interval shrinks and, in this continuous model, its probability also approaches zero.

Later, this idea will help us understand why the probability density, usually denoted by \(f_X(x)\), is not the probability of a point, that is,

\[ f_X(x) \neq P(X=x). \]

But the strangeness does not end here.

And does probability \(1\) mean absolute certainty?

Now consider the event

\[ A=[0,1]\setminus\{0.5\}. \]

In other words, any outcome is accepted, except exactly \(0.5\).

We already know that

\[ P(\{0.5\})=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ P(A) = P([0,1])-P(\{0.5\}) = 1-0 = 1. \]

So:

\[ \boxed{P(A)=1.} \]

But be careful:

\[ A\neq\Omega. \]

The outcome \(0.5\) still belongs to the sample space.

It is still a possible outcome of the experiment.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{P(A)=1\;\not\Rightarrow\;A=\Omega.} \]

Two counterintuitive facts

In continuous models, \(P(A)=0\) does not necessarily mean that \(A\) is impossible.

And \(P(A)=1\) does not necessarily mean that \(A\) literally contains all possible outcomes.

This is why, later on, we will encounter the expression almost surely: an event can occur with probability \(1\) even when there are possible outcomes, of total probability zero, in which it does not occur.

This is one of those situations in which everyday language and mathematical language begin to diverge.

In everyday life, we tend to use

“probability zero”

as synonymous with

“this will never happen”.

But mathematically, these two statements are different.

And in continuous models, this difference is more than a curiosity:

  • every trial ends at some individual point of probability zero. 😲

Returning to \(\mathcal F\): where \(P\) is defined

One final question remains.

Recall the construction we made:

  • \(\Omega\) contains all the possible elementary outcomes of the experiment;
  • an event \(A\subseteq\Omega\) represents a question about the experiment;
  • \(\mathcal F\) brings together precisely the questions we decided to consider in our probability model.

For example, if \(\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\), the event

\[ A=\{2,4,6\} \]

represents the question:

was the outcome even?

When we write

\[ A\in\mathcal F, \]

we are saying that this question belongs to the collection of events on which our probability function is defined.

In other words, we can ask for

\[ P(A). \]

This is also the intuitive interpretation of the word measurable.

An event \(A\) is measurable when \(A\in\mathcal F\): it is a set to which our probability measure \(P\) can assign a value.

In other words,

\[ A\in\mathcal F \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(A)\text{ is defined.} \]

In simple finite spaces, we can usually include all possible questions:

\[ \mathcal F=2^\Omega. \]

That is, every subset of \(\Omega\) can be treated as an event.

In continuous spaces, however, there are sufficiently irregular subsets for which we cannot define a probability measure while preserving all the properties we require of \(P\).

For this reason, rather than necessarily using all subsets of \(\Omega\), we choose a collection \(\mathcal F\) of measurable events.

But \(\mathcal F\) cannot be just any collection either.

Recall the idea of closure: if we can formulate certain questions about the experiment, we also want to be able to formulate questions naturally constructed from them.

For example, if \(A\in\mathcal F\) represents

“does the outcome belong to \(A\)?”,

we also want to be able to ask

“does the outcome not belong to \(A\)?”

Therefore,

\[ A\in\mathcal F \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^c\in\mathcal F. \]

Similarly, if we have a sequence of events \(A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots\) in \(\mathcal F\), we want to be able to ask

“did at least one of these events occur?”

This question corresponds to the union

\[ \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_i, \]

and therefore we require that

\[ A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots\in\mathcal F \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_i\in\mathcal F. \]

A family with these properties — and which contains \(\Omega\) — is called a \(\sigma\)-algebra.

The operational idea is simple:

\(\mathcal F\) must contain the questions we want to ask and must also contain the new questions we construct from them.

Thus, probabilistic operations never take us outside the domain of \(P\).

When \(\Omega\) lies on the real line: the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra

We can now connect this construction to the continuous case we are studying.

Suppose our sample space is

\[ \Omega=[0,1]. \]

Recall the interpretation we are using:

  • \(\Omega\) contains the possible outcomes;
  • an event is a question about the outcome of the experiment;
  • \(\mathcal F\) contains the questions for which we want \(P\) to be defined.

If the outcome is a real number, many of the most natural questions are described by intervals.

For example:

“was the outcome between \(0.2\) and \(0.5\)?”

corresponds to the event

\[ [0.2,0.5]. \]

Similarly, in real-world applications:

  • “was the time between 2 and 5 seconds?” corresponds to an interval;
  • “was the temperature above 30°C?” corresponds to an interval;
  • “was the spending less than R$ 100?” corresponds to an interval.

So, when working with quantities modeled on the real line, it seems natural to start our collection of events with intervals.

But we want more than just the original intervals.

If we can ask

“was the outcome between 2 and 5?”

we also want to be able to ask questions constructed from it, such as

“was the outcome outside this interval?”

or combine several regions of the real line into a single question.

For this reason, we start with intervals and close this collection under the operations required of a \(\sigma\)-algebra.

The collection obtained in this way is called the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra.

Intuitively:

the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra contains the sets we can construct from intervals on the real line by repeatedly using complements and countable unions.

It is the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing the intervals of the real line.

This means that we are not simply putting every imaginable subset of \(\mathbb R\) into \(\mathcal F\).

We start with extremely natural objects — intervals — and include everything we need to add so that the collection remains closed under probabilistic operations.

These sets are called Borel sets.

Thus, in a model such as

\[ \Omega=[0,1], \]

a natural choice is to work with the Borel sets contained in \([0,1]\).

The probability function is then defined on these events:

\[ P:\mathcal F\longrightarrow[0,1]. \]

The intuitive interpretation remains exactly the same:

\(\mathcal F\) organizes the questions about the experiment for which we want it to make sense to ask “what is the probability that this happens?”

We do not need to learn how to construct the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra formally in this course.

The important point is to understand why it appears: in continuous spaces, we need to choose carefully which sets will be measurable events, and intervals on the real line provide the natural starting point for this construction.

What about the real world?

This strange phenomenon appears very often in Data Science, but most people are not even aware of it!

Imagine we are building a churn model.

For each customer, we observe some characteristics and then whether the customer actually left or not.

A small portion of the dataset might look like this:

customer monthly spending (R$) hours in app/week tickets in the last 30 days observed churn (Y) estimated churn probability
A 187.43 6.72 0 0 0.08
B 94.18 1.37 3 1 0.71
C 231.06 3.94 1 0 0.22

Notice customer B.

After the experiment took place, we observed:

\[ x_B=(94.18,\;1.37,\;3,\ldots) \]

and we also observed that

\[ Y_B=1. \]

That row is in the dataset.

It happened.

Now suppose that at least some of these characteristics are modeled as continuous.

Then, for an exact feature vector \(x_B\),

\[ P(X=x_B=(94.18,\;1.37,\;3,\ldots))=0. \]

And if we treat the complete outcome for that customer as the pair

\[ (X,Y)=(x_B,1)=(94.18,\;1.37,\;3,\ldots,1), \]

we will also have, in this type of model,

\[ P(X=x_B,\;Y=1)=P(X=(94.18,\;1.37,\;3,\ldots),\;Y=1))=0. \]

Even so, it was precisely a realization of this kind that appeared before us in the dataset.

It is the same “madness” we have just seen in the interval:

the fact that an individual outcome has probability zero does not prevent it from being the observed outcome of a trial.

What changed?

The structure remains the same:

\[ \boxed{(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)}. \]

But we now better understand the role of each component, even when \(\Omega\) cannot be listed outcome by outcome:

  • \(\Omega\): which outcomes can occur;
  • \(\mathcal F\): which questions about these outcomes we can represent as measurable events;
  • \(P\): what probability we assign to each of these questions.

In the discrete case, we can often think of probability as mass placed on individual points.

In the continuous case, this intuition is no longer sufficient:

\[ \boxed{\text{discrete: mass on points}} \;\longrightarrow\; \boxed{\text{continuous: measure of sets}}. \]

This is the main conceptual leap of the chapter.

what happens when we repeat the experiment several times?

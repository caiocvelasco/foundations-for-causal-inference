Conditional Probability: Information, Updating Probabilities, and Bayes

In the chapter on IID, we used conditional probability to investigate whether knowing the result of one trial changes the probabilities associated with another.

Now let us study this idea in its own right:

When we receive new information, how should we update the probabilities of a model?

1. New information, a new question

We roll a fair die once. Before knowing the outcome, we ask what the probability is that it is even.

Let \(A=\{2,4,6\}\) (“even”) and \(B=\{4,5,6\}\) (“greater than 3”). Initially, \(P(A)=1/2\). We then receive the information that \(B\) has occurred: now, among \(4,5,6\), two outcomes are even. Thus,

\[ P(A)=\tfrac12,\qquad P(A\mid B)=\tfrac23. \]

We have neither repeated the experiment nor changed the die. We have changed the information available about the outcome.

2. The revised probability model

For events \(A,B\in\mathcal F\) with \(P(B)>0\), we define

\[ \boxed{P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.} \]

When we fix the event \(B\), the rule \(A\mapsto P(A\mid B)\) is a new probability law on the original events: the conditional probability law.

This is the meaning of the revised probability model emphasized by Tsitsiklis: now that we know that \(B\) has occurred, probabilities are calculated within \(B\) and appropriately normalized.

For example, \(P(B\mid B)=1\) and \(P(B^c\mid B)=0\).

3. Probabilities as beliefs: what does this language mean?

Tsitsiklis also uses the language of probabilistic beliefs: a probability expresses the degree of plausibility we assign to an event within a model.

Receiving new information may lead to revised beliefs, that is, conditional probabilities that differ from the initial probabilities.

It is important to distinguish the language of information updating from a philosophical claim: the conditional probability formula holds regardless of whether we adopt a frequentist or a subjective interpretation of probability.

4. The multiplication rule

The definition of conditioning implies:

\[ \boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B).} \]

If \(P(A)>0\), we can also write \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)\). This rule is useful for reasoning about processes that unfold in stages.

Exercise 1. An urn contains 3 red balls and 2 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. What is the probability that both are red? Write your solution using conditional probabilities, without assuming independence.

5. Total probability: different ways an event can occur

If \(B_1,\ldots,B_k\) form a partition of the space, with \(P(B_j)>0\), then

\[ \boxed{P(A)=\sum_{j=1}^k P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j).} \]

The law of total probability decomposes \(P(A)\) into mutually exclusive and exhaustive scenarios.

Exercise 2. A company ships 60% of its orders through carrier 1 and 40% through carrier 2. The probabilities of delay are 5% and 10%, respectively. What is the probability that a randomly selected order arrives late?

6. Bayes: from observed information to possible scenarios

Suppose we know the probabilities of observing evidence \(A\) under each scenario \(B_j\). After observing \(A\), we want to determine the probability of each scenario.

Bayes’ theorem combines the multiplication rule with the law of total probability:

\[ \boxed{P(B_j\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^kP(A\mid B_\ell)P(B_\ell)}},\qquad P(A)>0. \]

In the language of belief updating, \(P(B_j)\) is the prior probability, and \(P(B_j\mid A)\) is the posterior probability. Bayes’ theorem does not assume that probabilities are subjective: it is an identity that holds within the probability model.

Exercise 3. Only 2% of manufactured items are defective. A test flags 90% of genuinely defective items and 5% of nondefective items as defective. If the test flags an item as defective, what is the probability that the item is actually defective? Explain why the answer is not 90%.

7. Returning to independence

If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent and \(P(B)>0\), then \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\): the information that \(B\) has occurred does not change the probability of \(A\).

But conditioning is not synonymous with dependence. Conditioning is the general operation of updating probabilities when an event is known to have occurred; independence is a special property of the joint probability law.

Part 2 of 2 - Sampling Space

In the chapter on formalization, we began with the broader conceptual object: the Random Experiment.

It is the mechanism we want to represent mathematically:

\[ \boxed{\text{Random Experiment}} \longrightarrow \boxed{\text{mathematical representation}} \longrightarrow \boxed{\text{Statistical Space}} \]

In the framework we are building, the Statistical Space has two main components:

Statistical Space
Probability Space
what can happen and how we represent its uncertainty

\((\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\)

✓ already constructed
Sampling Space
how to represent repetition of the experiment

\(\mathcal G_n=\{T_1,\ldots,T_n\}\)

(we are here)

In the previous chapter, we opened the first box and constructed

\[ \boxed{ (\Omega,\mathcal F,P) } \]

to represent uncertainty about one complete trial of the experiment we defined.

Now we return to the overall framework and open the second box.

Where are we in the formalization of the Random Experiment?

Recall that the Random Experiment brings together three ideas:

  1. [a] we know the possible outcomes;
  2. [b] we do not know in advance which outcome will occur in a trial, but there is probabilistic regularity;
  3. [c] the experiment can be repeated under the same conditions.

Conditions [a] and [b] led us to the Probability Space.

Now we begin to formalize [c].

But there is an important subtlety: before explaining what it means to repeat an experiment under the same conditions, we must first construct a structure capable of representing several trials jointly.

That is precisely the objective of this chapter.

We will begin with a general sequence

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal G_n=\{T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_n\} } \]

without yet assuming independence or identical distributions.

At the same time, we will need to extend

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal F,P) \]

to a probabilistic structure that can represent the outcomes of these trials jointly:

\[ \boxed{ (\Omega^{(n)},\mathcal F^{(n)},P^{(n)}). } \]

By the end of this chapter, we will have constructed the General Statistical Space:

\[ \boxed{ \left[ (\Omega^{(n)},\mathcal F^{(n)},P^{(n)}), \mathcal G_n \right]. } \]

Only in the next step will we ask:

what does it mean, mathematically, to repeat this experiment “under the same conditions”?

That is where we will specialize \(\mathcal G_n\) by imposing Identical Distribution + Independence, producing

\[ \mathcal G_n^{IID}, \]

a sequence of Random Trials.

Therefore, the step taken in this chapter is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{one trial} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{several trials represented jointly} } \]

and not yet

\[ \boxed{ \text{several trials} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{IID}. } \]

Now we can construct this second box carefully.

The form of \(\Omega\) depends on how we define the experiment!

In the previous chapter, we saw that the sample space must represent everything we want to observe at the end of one trial.

For this reason, the number of coordinates of an outcome \(\omega\) depends on how we define the experiment.

If the experiment is

tossing a coin once,

we have \(\Omega=\{H,T\}\) and one possible outcome is simply \(\omega=H\).

But we could define another experiment:

tossing a coin twice and recording the order.

Now a trial ends only after both tosses, and one possible outcome is \(\omega=(H,T)\).

This is still a single outcome of a single trial.

Mathematically, the sample space can be written as

\[ \Omega = \{H,T\}\times\{H,T\}=\{(H,H),(H,T),(T,H),(T,T)\}. \]

The Cartesian product simply constructs a space whose elements have several coordinates.

The coordinates alone do not tell us whether trials were repeated. Their interpretation comes from how we define the experiment.

The context of this chapter

Now we will make a specific choice.

We will define our basic experiment as:

tossing a coin once.

Therefore, one trial of this experiment produces just one \(\omega\in\Omega\), that is, an element of \(\{H,T\}\).

From this point on, whenever we perform this same basic experiment again, we will treat each new execution as a new trial.

It is in this sense, and only because we have chosen this experiment boundary, that the coordinates appearing below will have a new interpretation:

\[ (\omega_1,\omega_2,\ldots,\omega_n) \]

will represent the outcomes of different trials.

From one trial to several trials

We represent each trial by \(T_i\):

\[ T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_n. \]

Each trial produces its own outcome:

\[ T_1\to\omega_1, \qquad T_2\to\omega_2, \qquad \ldots, \qquad T_n\to\omega_n. \]

We now want to represent these outcomes jointly.

This is the new idea in this chapter:

\[ \boxed{ \text{one trial} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{several trials of the same basic experiment} } \]

Why is \((\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\) alone not enough?

For one coin toss, \(\Omega=\{H,T\}\).

Since the space is finite, we can take

\[ \mathcal F = \left\{ \varnothing, \{H\}, \{T\}, \{H,T\} \right\}. \]

Thus, we can ask questions such as

“did we get heads?”

represented by the event \(A=\{H\}\in\mathcal F\), and calculate \(P(A)\).

But now we want to ask:

what is the probability of obtaining exactly three heads in five trials?

An outcome relevant to this question has the form

\[ (H,T,H,H,T), \]

and

\[ (H,T,H,H,T)\notin\Omega. \]

This object simply does not live in the space we constructed for one trial.

The problem is the domain

The function

\[ P:\mathcal F\to[0,1] \]

only assigns probabilities to events in \(\mathcal F\).

But \(\mathcal F\) was constructed over the outcome space of one trial.

To discuss five trials, we must first construct a space in which sequences of five outcomes exist.

Where do the outcomes of the sequence live?

For five trials, we construct

\[ \Omega^5 = \Omega\times\Omega\times\Omega\times\Omega\times\Omega. \]

Now

\[ \omega = (\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\omega_4,\omega_5) = (H,T,H,H,T) \]

is a legitimate element of this space:

\[ \omega\in\Omega^5. \]

We can visualize the joint trials of this experiment as follows:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{ccccc} T_1 & T_2 & T_3 & T_4 & T_5\\ \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow\\ H & T & H & H & T \end{array} } \]

The Cartesian product constructs all possible joint realizations of the sequence.

In general, we will denote this joint sample space by

\[ \boxed{ \Omega^{(n)} = \Omega_1\times\cdots\times\Omega_n } \quad \text{and} \quad (\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n)\in\Omega^{(n)} \]

If all trials have the same sample space \(\Omega\), for simplicity, we write

\[ \Omega^{(n)}=\Omega^n. \]

From the product space to the joint probability space

As with one trial, the set of possible outcomes is not enough.

We also need:

  • an event space \(\mathcal F^{(n)}\);
  • a joint law \(P^{(n)}:\mathcal F^{(n)}\to[0,1]\).

We thus arrive at the joint probability space

\[ \boxed{ (\Omega^{(n)},\mathcal F^{(n)},P^{(n)}) } \]

Returning to three heads in five trials

We can now formally define

\[ B = \left\{ (\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_5)\in\Omega^5: \text{ exactly three coordinates are }H \right\}. \]

For example,

\[ (H,T,H,H,T)\in B, \]

whereas

\[ (H,T,H,T,T)\notin B. \]

Now \(B\) is indeed an event of the sequence, that is,

\[ B\in\mathcal F^{(5)}, \]

and the original question becomes “what is the probability of \(B\)”

\[ P^{(5)}(B). \]

The idea of probability has not changed

For one trial:

\[ A\in\mathcal F \quad\longrightarrow\quad P(A). \]

For several trials:

\[ B\in\mathcal F^{(n)} \quad\longrightarrow\quad P^{(n)}(B). \]

What changed was the space in which the events live.

A notational convention

Strictly speaking, \((H,T)\) is a possible outcome and

\[ \{(H,T)\} \]

is the singleton event containing that outcome.

Thus, the explicit form is

\[ P^{(2)}\big(\{(H,T)\}\big). \]

Having made this clear, to simplify notation, we will write

\[ P^{(2)}(H,T) \]

as shorthand, leaving the braces implicit.

Knowing each trial separately is not enough

Let us return to the case of two trials of the basic experiment:

\[ T_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad T_2. \]

Suppose that, when we look at each trial separately, both are described as a fair coin:

\[ P_1(\{H\})=P_1(\{T\})=\frac12, \qquad P_2(\{H\})=P_2(\{T\})=\frac12. \]

That is, we know the distribution of the outcome of \(T_1\) and the distribution of the outcome of \(T_2\).

We can summarize this in the table below:

outcome \(P_1\) \(P_2\)
\(H\) \(1/2\) \(1/2\)
\(T\) \(1/2\) \(1/2\)

The two distributions are therefore identical.

This means that if we look at each trial in isolation, both behave like a fair coin.

But this still does not answer the central question:

how do the outcomes of \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) occur together?

This is precisely why we still need the joint law \(P^{(2)}\).

Knowing the probabilities of each trial in isolation does not, by itself, determine the probabilities of the four possible sequences \((H,H),(H,T),(T,H),(T,T)\).

This is why we still need to specify a joint law, which we will denote by \(P^{(2)}\).

And different joint laws can be compatible with exactly the same individual probabilities above.

Here we can revisit an idea introduced at the beginning of the course:

  • behind the observed outcomes there is an underlying mechanism, which we call the Data Generating Process (DGP).

We are still working at the abstract level of trials and outcomes, but the interpretation is already the same:

The DGP determines how trial outcomes can occur jointly. The law \(P^{(2)}\) is our probabilistic representation of this mechanism that generates the outcomes of an experiment in the form of a sequence of two trials.

The two cases below therefore correspond to two different generating mechanisms.

Case 1: two ordinary tosses of a fair coin

What is the experiment?

We define our experiment as a sequence of two trials:

\[ T_1 \quad\text{followed by}\quad T_2. \]

Each trial can result in \(H\) or \(T\). Therefore, the outcome of the complete experiment is an ordered pair:

\[ \Omega^{(2)} = \{(H,H),(H,T),(T,H),(T,T)\}. \]

So far, we know only which sequences are possible.

Now we need to specify how the DGP distributes probability among these sequences.

Suppose that the mechanism corresponds to what we normally imagine as two separate tosses of a fair coin: there is no mechanism causing the outcome of one toss to alter the other.

In this case, we model the four sequences as equally likely. Since their probabilities must sum to \(1\),

\[ P^{(2)}(H,H) = P^{(2)}(H,T) = P^{(2)}(T,H) = P^{(2)}(T,T) = \frac14. \]

The table simply organizes this law:

\(\begin{array}{c} T_2\rightarrow \\ T_1\downarrow \end{array}\) \(H\) \(T\) total for \(T_1\)
\(H\) \(1/4\) \(1/4\) \(1/2\)
\(T\) \(1/4\) \(1/4\) \(1/2\)
total for \(T_2\) \(1/2\) \(1/2\) \(1\)

How do we interpret a cell?

Consider

\[ P^{(2)}(H,T)=\frac14. \]

This means:

the probability that the complete experiment produces the sequence “\(H\) on the first trial and \(T\) on the second trial” is \(1/4\).

The \(1/4\) is therefore the probability of one joint outcome:

\[ (H,T). \]

We are not assigning \(1/4\) to \(H\) and \(1/4\) to \(T\) separately.

When we look only at \(T_1\) or only at \(T_2\), we recover

\[ P_1(H)=P_1(T)=\frac12, \qquad P_2(H)=P_2(T)=\frac12. \]

But \(P^{(2)}\) contains something more: it tells us how the outcomes of the two trials occur together.

Case 2: the same outcome space, but without assuming independence

What is the experiment?

We retain exactly the same experiment: a sequence of two trials,

\[ T_1 \quad\text{followed by}\quad T_2, \]

where each trial can result in \(H\) or \(T\).

Therefore, the outcome space is still

\[ \Omega^{(2)} = \{(H,H),(H,T),(T,H),(T,T)\}. \]

What changes now is not the outcome space, but the generating mechanism.

In Case 1, we constructed a DGP in which the two trials behaved like separate tosses of a fair coin.

Now we want to show that this need not be true.

We can have a DGP in which the trials are related, so that knowing the outcome of one provides information about the other.

In other words, we are not yet assuming that the trials are independent.

To make this particularly clear, let us construct an extreme case.

Suppose that the DGP chooses \(H\) or \(T\) with probability \(1/2\) on the first trial and makes the second trial copy the first.

Thus, if the first outcome is \(H\), the complete outcome will be

\[ (H,H), \]

and if the first outcome is \(T\),

\[ (T,T). \]

The sequences \((H,T)\) and \((T,H)\) never occur under this mechanism.

The joint law is then:

\(\begin{array}{c} T_2\rightarrow \\ T_1\downarrow \end{array}\) \(H\) \(T\) total for \(T_1\)
\(H\) \(1/2\) \(0\) \(1/2\)
\(T\) \(0\) \(1/2\) \(1/2\)
total for \(T_2\) \(1/2\) \(1/2\) \(1\)

How do we interpret a cell?

Consider

\[ P^{(2)}(H,T)=0. \]

This means:

under this DGP, the probability that the complete experiment produces the sequence “\(H\) on the first trial and \(T\) on the second trial” is zero.

Now notice something important.

If we look at each trial in isolation, we still have

\[ P_1(H)=P_1(T)=\frac12, \qquad P_2(H)=P_2(T)=\frac12. \]

That is, each trial still looks like a fair coin when observed separately.

But when we look at both jointly, we see information that these individual probabilities do not reveal:

if we know that \(T_1\) resulted in \(H\), we also know that \(T_2\) resulted in \(H\).

Therefore, what changed was the way the two outcomes occur jointly.

We are not yet talking about two random variables

These tables resemble a joint distribution of two random variables, but we are still one step before that.

Here they represent the joint law \(P^{(2)}\) over the possible outcomes of the two trials:

\[ \Omega^{(2)} = \{(H,H),(H,T),(T,H),(T,T)\}. \]

Later, after introducing random variables \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), this same idea will reappear as a joint distribution of random variables.

This can also occur in real-world phenomena

Imagine two parts produced in sequence by the same machine.

If the machine is temporarily out of adjustment, observing a defect in the first part may provide information about the chance that the second part is also defective.

This does not necessarily mean that the defect in the first part causes the defect in the second. The two outcomes may be related because they share part of the same generating mechanism.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{probabilities of each trial in isolation} \;\not\Rightarrow\; \text{joint law of the sequence} } \]

It is precisely the joint law \(P^{(n)}\) that contains information about how the outcomes of different trials occur together.

The second ingredient: sampling space

Now we give a name to the sequence of trials:

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal G_n=\{T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_n\}. } \]

We will call \(\mathcal G_n\) the sampling space.

It identifies the trials we are representing jointly.

Combining

\[ (\Omega^{(n)},\mathcal F^{(n)},P^{(n)}) \]

and

\[ \mathcal G_n, \]

we obtain the general statistical space:

\[ \boxed{ \left[ (\Omega^{(n)},\mathcal F^{(n)},P^{(n)}), \mathcal G_n \right]. } \]

The sample space is not the sampling space

The sample space \(\Omega\) contains possible outcomes.

The sampling space \(\mathcal G_n\) identifies the sequence of trials under consideration.

And we are not yet talking about a random sample.

That will come later, when we introduce random variables.

What if \(\Omega\) is continuous?

Nothing changes conceptually.

If \(\Omega=\mathbb R\), then \(n\) trials produce elements of

\[ \mathbb R^n. \]

The rigorous construction of the joint event space uses the product \(\sigma\)-algebra

\[ \mathcal F_1\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal F_n, \]

but we will not develop this technical part here.

The idea remains the same: several trials must live within a joint probabilistic structure.

What is still missing?

So far, we have not assumed that the trials are independent.

Nor have we assumed that they all have the same distribution.

We have only constructed the structure needed to say:

\[ \boxed{ \text{these trials will be represented jointly} } \]

and allow \(P^{(n)}\) to describe how they are related.

In the next chapter, we will ask an additional question:

what do we mean when we say that the same mechanism is repeated “under the same conditions”?

This statement will lead us to two different ideas:

  • the same probabilistic structure across trials;
  • the absence of probabilistic influence between them.

To formalize the second, we must first understand an even more basic question:

how does new information change a probability?

This is where conditional probability comes in.

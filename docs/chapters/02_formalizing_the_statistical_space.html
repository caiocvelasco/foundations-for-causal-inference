Mathematical Formalization - Statistical Space

In the previous chapter, we saw why we need Probability.

Now the formalization begins: we will turn the idea of an uncertain phenomenon into a mathematical structure that can later be used to model observed data.

Formalizing is not simply replacing words with symbols. It is learning to state precisely:

  • what we are modeling;
  • which assumptions we are imposing;
  • which objects are part of the model;
  • and how these objects relate to one another.

Modeling begins before choosing an estimator, testing a hypothesis, or running an algorithm.

From the Random Experiment to the Statistical Space

The starting point is the Random Experiment: the conceptual mechanism we want to represent mathematically.

In Spanos’s framework, it brings together three ideas:

  1. [a] we know the possible outcomes a priori;
  2. [b] in a particular trial, we do not know which outcome will occur, but there is probabilistic regularity;
  3. [c] the experiment can be repeated under the same conditions.

The formalization of these ideas will give rise to the Statistical Space.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Random Experiment} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Statistical Space} } \]

In other words, the Random Experiment is not “inside” the Statistical Space. It is the conceptual phenomenon we are trying to represent mathematically.

The first part: Probability Space

Conditions [a] and [b] are formalized by the Probability Space.

For a single trial:

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal F,P). \]

Here:

  • \(\Omega\) contains the possible outcomes;
  • \(\mathcal F\) organizes the questions we want to ask about these outcomes;
  • \(P\) assigns probabilities to these questions.

When we want to represent several trials jointly, we use the corresponding structure

\[ (\Omega^{(n)},\mathcal F^{(n)},P^{(n)}). \]

The idea is the same. We are now describing the possible outcomes of the \(n\) trials jointly in probabilistic terms.

The second part: Sampling Space

Condition [c] introduces a second structure.

If we repeat the experiment \(n\) times, we write

\[ \mathcal G_n=\{T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_n\}, \]

where \(T_i\) represents the \(i\)th trial.

We will call \(\mathcal G_n\) the Sampling Space.

At this point, it simply organizes the sequence of trials.

We are not yet assuming that these trials are independent or identically distributed.

Therefore:

  • \(T_i\) = trial;
  • \(\omega_i\) = outcome of trial \(T_i\);
  • \(\Omega\) = sample space;
  • \(\mathcal G_n\) = sampling space.

These last two objects have similar names, but they represent different things.

Putting the two parts together

We can now see the complete mathematical representation.

mathematical representation
\(\downarrow\)
Statistical Space
Probability Space

\((\Omega^{(n)},\mathcal F^{(n)},P^{(n)})\)

Thus, in the general case:

\[ \boxed{ \left[ (\Omega^{(n)},\mathcal F^{(n)},P^{(n)}), \quad \mathcal G_n \right] } \]

is the abstract structure we will use to represent uncertainty and repetition of the experiment jointly.

Preview: when trials are IID

So far, \(\mathcal G_n\) represents only a sequence of trials.

Later, we will formalize the idea of repeating the experiment under the same conditions by imposing two properties:

  • Identical Distribution (ID): the same probabilistic structure is maintained from trial to trial;
  • Independence (I): information about one trial does not alter the probabilities of the others.

Just to be clear: in Mathematics and Statistics, imposing properties or making assumptions about a space or model means restricting the possibilities it allows. In other words, we are specializing a more general structure.

By imposing Identical Distribution and Independence on the Sampling Space, we obtain a simpler and more restrictive case of the Statistical Space.

Why do we do this? Because starting with a simple structure makes it easier for us to understand and study the phenomenon.

Later, when these assumptions are not appropriate, we can relax them and construct more general structures capable of representing dependence, heterogeneity, and other patterns found in the data.

Visually:

\[ \mathcal G_n \quad\xrightarrow{\text{ID + Independence}}\quad \mathcal G_n^{IID}. \]

The sequence \(\mathcal G_n^{IID}\) will be called a sequence of Random Trials.

In this simple case, the Statistical Space takes the form

\[ \boxed{ \left[ (\Omega,\mathcal F,P)^n, \mathcal G_n^{IID} \right] } \]

which we will call the Simple Statistical Space.

We do not need to develop IID now.

For now, just remember the architecture:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Random Experiment} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Statistical Space} } \]

with two parts:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Probability Space} \;+\; \text{Sampling Space} } \]

In the next chapters, we will open these boxes more carefully and then transform this abstract structure into objects suitable for working with data.

Before we finish: Feynman and the framework

Before moving on, watch this short video by Richard Feynman.

The important point here is the idea that an explanation can only move forward when the person asking the question and the person answering it share a framework of knowledge and reasoning.

He discusses this around the 1:38 mark.

This is exactly what we are doing now: building the necessary language so that, later on, concepts in Statistics and Causal Inference do not appear as isolated formulas.
